## Rock is a starting surface, not a finished soil

**Big question:** how can a surface that appears empty begin to support living communities?

Think back to the patch with exposed rock. A plant needs more than a place on a map: it needs suitable physical conditions, access to water and nutrients, and a way to arrive. Fresh volcanic material can provide a starting surface for ecological development, but "volcanic" does not mean that a deep, fertile soil appears immediately. Soil development involves changes in both mineral material and organic matter over time.

Physical weathering breaks material into smaller pieces. Water, temperature changes and other environmental processes can contribute to this breakdown. Cracks and hollows can trap fine material carried by wind or water. As organisms become established, their remains add organic material. These processes interact; they do not form a simple conveyor belt that moves every surface through identical stages at identical speeds.

1. **Material changes:** rock weathers and fine particles accumulate.
2. **Organisms arrive:** suitable microsites provide opportunities to establish.
3. **Conditions change again:** living organisms and organic matter influence their surroundings.

> [!TIP]
> Treat this list as interacting processes, not a fixed timetable. The order and speed can vary.

## Pioneers and small opportunities

The term pioneer describes organisms that can become established early in a developing community. It is a description of their role in a particular setting, not a promise that one named species must always arrive first. Lichens, mosses and other organisms can be useful examples when learning about colonisation of volcanic surfaces. Which organisms actually establish depends on local conditions and on which propagules reach a suitable place.

A microsite is a small location whose conditions differ from its surroundings. A sheltered crack may retain fine material or moisture differently from an exposed ridge of rock. A seed does not experience the average conditions of an entire mountain; it experiences conditions where it lands. This is why a surface that looks almost empty from a distance can still contain small patches of life when studied carefully.

As vegetation develops, it can alter its surroundings. Plant litter contributes organic matter, roots occupy cracks, and the ground beneath vegetation can differ from nearby exposed ground. Those changes may make some conditions more suitable for other organisms, although competition can also increase. Ecological development includes interactions and setbacks, not only a one-directional increase in the number of plants.

## Work through a colonisation puzzle

Allow about two minutes for this invented comparison. Two small areas of the same exposed rock surface are equally old. Area A is a wind-exposed ridge. Area B is a sheltered crack containing fine material. After a season, you observe more seedlings in B. Write two explanations that could fit the observation. Then decide what you would measure to distinguish between them.

- [ ] Suggest two explanations for the different seedling counts.
- [ ] Choose a measurement for each explanation.

::: reveal Explore two possible explanations

One explanation is that the crack retains moisture more effectively. Another is that it collects more seeds or fine material. Both could be operating, and neither is proved by the seedling count alone. Comparing moisture and the arrival of seeds would address different parts of the explanation. Repeating the comparison at several cracks and ridges would make it less dependent on one unusual location. These are examples of testable ideas, not measurements from an actual Etna study.

:::

## Avoid the automatic-timetable mistake

It is tempting to turn succession into a fixed schedule: bare rock in year one, a certain plant in year ten, and a forest in year one hundred. That is not the rule taught here. Conditions differ, disturbances recur and chance arrival can matter. An older exposed patch can remain sparsely vegetated, while a protected microsite on another surface can support plants earlier. Surface age is one variable among several.

Replace ~~one guaranteed timetable~~ with **an explanation that accounts for local conditions**.

For the quiz, remember the relationships. Rock breakdown and accumulated organic matter contribute to soil development. Microsites can differ in moisture, shelter and trapped material. Early colonisers exploit suitable conditions, but no universal sequence or timetable is guaranteed.

## Elevation is part of the explanation

Etna's living landscape varies with more than the age of its volcanic surfaces. Elevation and the direction a slope faces can influence the conditions found there. The official park description connects vegetation patterns with differences in substrate, temperature, precipitation, altitude and exposure. Those factors help explain why one mountain can contain contrasting habitats rather than a single uniform plant community.

Avoid treating elevation as a magic label. Saying "this plot is higher" describes its position. An ecological explanation asks what changes for organisms at that position. Temperature, moisture and exposure are possible parts of the answer. Likewise, two slopes at similar elevations can differ in their exposure to sun and wind. A useful explanation connects a location to the conditions experienced by living things.

## Make a fair comparison

Suppose you want to test whether a particular kind of sheltered microsite supports more seedlings than exposed ground. If all your sheltered observations come from low slopes and all your exposed observations come from much higher slopes, the comparison mixes shelter with elevation. You would not know which difference contributed to the result. This is a confounding problem: more than one changing factor could explain the pattern.

A stronger design compares sheltered and exposed microsites under otherwise similar conditions, then repeats that comparison in several places. Record the features that might matter instead of selecting only the plots that fit your expectation. Repeated observations cannot remove every uncertainty, but they make your reasoning more transparent and help you recognise exceptions.

| Compare | Keep the method consistent |
| --- | --- |
| Sheltered and exposed microsites | Similar elevation band |
| Several plots, not just one pair | Same counting method |
| Expected and unexpected results | Record both |

## Observation, inference and confidence

Imagine that three sheltered plots contain more seedlings than three exposed plots. That is a pattern in your small sample. It supports further investigation of shelter, but it is not proof that shelter alone controls all vegetation on Etna. The observations could reflect differences in seed arrival, surface history or other conditions you have not measured. The appropriate conclusion should be narrower than the most exciting story you can tell.

Good scientific notes also preserve unexpected results. If one sheltered plot has no seedlings, do not remove it simply because it makes the pattern less tidy. Ask whether it reveals a useful difference, a measurement issue or normal variation. An exception can help you identify the limits of an explanation. "I do not yet know" is a useful statement when it is paired with a clear next question.

If the counts differ, I will conclude that shelter caused the difference." Identify the confounded factors and write one practical improvement to the comparison. You are designing a desk-based example, not planning an actual visit.

::: reveal Check your study design

A better proposal would compare several sheltered and exposed plots within a comparable elevation band, record their surface histories where known, and use the same counting method in each plot. Shelter and elevation would no longer change together by design. The result would still describe your sampled plots and conditions, not every part of the mountain or every future year.

:::

For the quiz, focus on fair comparisons. More than one environmental factor can change at once. Repetition and consistent methods strengthen an observation, while selective removal of inconvenient results weakens it. A correlation is a pattern that needs interpretation; it does not automatically identify a single cause.
